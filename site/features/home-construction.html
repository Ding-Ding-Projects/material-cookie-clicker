Building the home

You live over the shop. Buy the drawing, pay the builders, and then wait — a room takes real minutes to put up. Furnish it afterwards, and the house pays you back a little for being a nice place to be.

Current state

Shipped The Home is its own console panel, opened by the Property Deed upgrade; the subgame lives in src/shared/game/home-construction.ts.

The second game inside the game

The diesel factory is a production line: things flow through it and stall against each other. This is not that. This is a house, and it is built the way a house is really built — one room at a time, in an order you choose, and each room takes real time to put up.

The whole loop is four steps and there is no fifth:

  1. Buy a room's blueprint with cookies. It buys the right to build, and nothing else.
  2. Pay the builders to start construction on it.
  3. Wait. Construction advances on elapsed game time and finishes when the time has genuinely been served.
  4. Furnish the finished room, a piece at a time, each piece carrying a coziness score and usually a small bonus.

Cookies touch the house in exactly those three purchases — blueprint, builders, furniture — and nothing here ever pays cookies back out.

From the running application

Three states, three drawings. A room you could buy the plan for is drawn as the plan: dashed walls, a door swing, a dimension line. A room going up is drawn with scaffolding across it and carries the real bar and the real countdown. A finished room is drawn as a floor with its furniture standing on it.

The six rooms

The Kitchen is first and is the only one that is first — this is a bakery, and a bakery with no kitchen is a shop with nothing to sell. Everything else hangs off the Kitchen being built, not merely bought, and is then taken in whatever order you like. A player who wants a Garden before a Bedroom is not wrong, only slower to sleep.

Costs climb about an order of magnitude a room against a doubling of minutes, so the last room is a genuine project rather than a long till receipt. The whole house is sixty-eight minutes of construction, and that is before any of it is furnished.

One site, one crew

Only one room is ever under construction. Starting a second while the first is up is refused outright — not queued behind it, not silently dropped in its place. The panel says so in words rather than leaving a disabled button to imply it, because a queue nobody asked for is a queue that spends cookies while the player is looking somewhere else.

The time is real, and it is only real time

Construction advances on the elapsed milliseconds the game loop hands it, exactly as the factory's tick does. No start timestamp is stored and no clock is consulted, so winding the system clock forward buys nothing. For the same reason a build does not advance while the application is closed: offline progress credits cookies and touches neither subgame. The builders keep the same hours the refinery does. The panel prints the time genuinely remaining, never an estimate.

The required time is frozen at the moment a build starts, after every build-speed bonus the house owns at that instant. Buying a Joiner's Bench halfway through a build does not retroactively shorten the build it is not helping with — and the countdown you are watching never jumps backwards.

Twenty-six pieces of furniture

Four to five pieces per room, twenty-six in all, each buyable once and only into a room that is actually built. Prices rise across the house rather than within a room, so a freshly finished Garden is a shopping trip rather than a shopping list with the first item greyed out.

What a piece can carry, on top of its coziness:

  • Production — eleven pieces, 1.5% to 4% each, multiplied into total cookies per second.
  • Click — four pieces, 3% to 6% each. Together they are ×1.1922 on a click.
  • Builders' pace — four pieces, shortening future builds by 5%, 8%, 12% and 15%. They add to 40%, and are capped at 60% so that adding a fifth later cannot produce an instant house.
  • Nothing at all — seven pieces are only there to be nice, and say so on the card. The Wall of Jars is the proudest wall in the house and does nothing.

The coziness curve

Every built room contributes its shell's coziness; every piece of furniture contributes its own. Nothing else contributes anything — coziness is not a function of cookies, of time, or of how long you have been playing. The total feeds one logarithmic curve:

multiplier = 1 + 0.06 × ln(1 + coziness / 40)

Logarithmic deliberately. A linear curve would make the last few pieces — the ones costing tens of millions — worth exactly as much as the first ones, which is wrong as an economy and wrong as a feeling: the difference between a bare room and a chair is enormous, and the difference between nine nice things and ten is small.

The curve is not the whole of what a furnished house pays. The eleven production pieces multiply to ×1.3505, and the panel folds both into the single figure it prints, so the honest ceiling is ×1.5108 on production for six rooms, thirty-eight purchases (six blueprints, six constructions, twenty-six pieces of furniture), 405,780,000 cookies and sixty-eight minutes of building.

Half again on production sounds large until you put it beside the main economy. It is deliberately of the same order as one of the cookie side's own global upgrades — Sturdier Ovens is ×1.1, Industrial-Grade Yeast ×1.25 — and there are a dozen of those, alongside a twenty-tier ladder and a prestige multiplier. Finishing the entire house is worth about as much as one good upgrade, spread across the entire mid-game. That is the point: the main economy stays primary, and stays primary by an enormous margin.

Nothing unlocks itself

The house arrives with the Property Deed, an upgrade costing 2,000 cookies and chained behind the Shop Sign — a player who has not yet found the shop has no business buying property. Buying the deed gives you a building and nothing inside it. Every room after that is a blueprint you buy, a construction you pay for, and a wait you actually serve. No room, no piece of furniture and no blueprint is ever granted, by progress, by a milestone, or by loading an old save.

A save written before the house existed reads back with an empty plot — no blueprints, no rooms, nothing being built, nothing invested — and no deed. It buys the deed like everyone else.

What is not photographed

Two things. The later rooms: no capture run has reached the Bedroom, Workshop or Garden, so the twenty- and thirty-minute builds and the fifteen most expensive pieces of furniture are unit-tested and undrawn. And the build-speed bonus doing its job — the builders' pace tile reads +0% in both captures, because the four pieces that raise it live in rooms this run never built. Those are gaps in evidence, not features known to be broken.
