Random events

Twenty-two events on a bounded random schedule — big frenzies, a Clot that halves production, a delivery chain, a two-button choice, an eclipse you have to search in the dark — and, rarely, two or three of them at once. Plus a Mouse Raid on its own hourly clock that can take up to 80% of your cookies if you do not whack the mice. Photographed running.

Current state

Shipped the scheduler, all twenty-two pool events, double and triple events, and the Mouse Raid are in the build (src/shared/game/random-events.ts). Four surfaces on this page are photographs: a Sugar Rush, a double event, a Cookie Eclipse, and a Mouse Raid in progress. The other nineteen events are described from their shipped definitions rather than from a picture, and the table below says outright which is which rather than letting a reader assume every row was seen.

From the running application

Fifteen of the sixteen have no photograph on this build. They are described below from their shipped definitions, and the table says so rather than implying a picture exists. Older photographs of a Cookie Rain, an Oven Hiccup, a Production Frenzy and a Taste Test were taken in earlier lanes and are still on disk, described in captures/README.md; they are not shown here because the surface around them has changed since.

What it does

Every few minutes, a spawn draws from a pool of twenty-two and interrupts an ordinary session. Most pay out, four cost you something for a while, one asks you a question with two buttons, and six of them put real buttons on the game stage that you can press. Usually that spawn brings exactly one event. About four times in a hundred it brings two, and about eight times in a thousand it brings three — and which events are allowed to share a stage is a stated, tested matrix rather than a coincidence. See Double and triple events.

The four that cost you something, stated plainly

Fifteen draws in every hundred are an event that makes your counter climb more slowly, and this page is not going to bury them under the good ones. The Oven Hiccup drops production to 40% for thirty seconds and gives you a button that ends it immediately. The Flour Shortage halves production for thirty seconds and then pays forty-five seconds’ worth in one lump when it ends — you come out ahead, but for half a minute you really are producing less. If a golden frenzy was running while the bins were empty, the dip cost you frenzy-scaled seconds, so the rebound is paid at that same rate rather than at the standing one; coming out ahead is a promise, not an average. The Clot halves production for sixty-six seconds and there is nothing at all you can do about it. Static Cling is the newest and the only one aimed somewhere else: it makes every click worth 35% of its value for forty seconds and leaves production completely alone.

Static Cling exists because until it shipped, a player who clicked for a living had never once been inconvenienced by bad weather — every penalty in the game reduced production, which is the thing an idle save cares about. Between it and Night Shift the two playstyles now have one event each aimed squarely at them. The Clot exists for a different reason and it is the reason the frenzies are worth anything: a pool whose every face is a present is not weather, it is a drip feed, and you stop reading the marquee.

None of the four touches your balance or your lifetime total. The one thing in this game that can take cookies you already have is the Mouse Raid, below. And two setbacks can never run together — that is the first rule of the stacking matrix, and it is there so that a coincidence can never turn two survivable events into a punishment.

How the multipliers stack

There are exactly two sources of a live multiplier: a golden cookie’s frenzy, and whatever this pool is currently running — which, since double and triple events, can be up to three things at once.

  • They multiply. A Production Frenzy inside a golden frenzy is ×7 by ×7 = ×49. Two effects that arrived independently both apply; taking one away because the other landed would punish you for good luck.
  • Penalties multiply too, and are never capped. A Clot during a golden frenzy is ×7 by 0.5 = ×3.5 — still a good minute, and visibly a worse one than it would have been.
  • The upside is capped, at a stated ceiling — and the ceilings did not move when triples arrived. Combined production is capped at ×1000 and combined click value at ×10000. They were always written as ceilings on the combined figure rather than on any one contributor, so they already described this situation before it existed, and raising them because more things can now multiply would be quietly loosening the only stated limit in the system. The biggest production stack the matrix permits is a Burnt Batch Frenzy with a Grandma Convention and a Baker’s Dozen — the frenzy is the only large factor, since neither companion multiplies global production at all — inside a golden frenzy, which is ×4662 and is capped to ×1000. Both ceilings stay far above anything one event reaches alone, so hitting one always means rare things coincided.
  • The cap is applied once, at the end. Not pairwise as the product is folded up. Capping a pair early and then multiplying by a Clot would clip a figure that was never over the ceiling in the first place, so there is one product and one comparison, and the answer cannot depend on the order the events are held in.
  • Rebates add rather than multiply. Market Day (15%) and a Baker’s Dozen (7.69%) together hand back 22.69% of a purchase, not 21.85%. Each event independently promises you a slice of what you paid, so you get both slices. There is a stated ceiling of 50% on the combined figure, far above anything two events can reach, so that no future combination can ever make a purchase free.
  • Sugar Rush is not special. It is a pool event like any other and goes through exactly the same rule.

The whole matrix is under test: every event against nothing, against a golden frenzy and against a golden click frenzy, including the two combinations that reach a ceiling and the ones that go the other way.

How often, and how that changed

The gap between two events is a flat one-minute cooldown plus a rolled delay. When the pool held six events that delay was three to ten minutes, which worked out at about eight events an hour. With sixteen events it is four to twelve minutes, which is about 6.7 an hour — nearly three times as many faces and fewer interruptions, because more variety is supposed to make each event rarer rather than make the session busier. At that rate a Production Frenzy lands about once every 3.7 hours, a Click Frenzy about once every five, and a Burnt Batch Frenzy about once every fifteen.

The draw is weighted, not uniform, and the weights above are literally the numbers in the code — they sum to exactly one hundred, which a test pins down, so a weight reads as a percentage without any arithmetic. A long seeded run asserts each event really is drawn at its stated rate.

Double and triple events

About four spawns in a hundred put two events on screen at once, and about eight in a thousand put up three. At six spawns an hour that is a double roughly every four hours of play and a triple roughly every twenty-one — rare enough that the marquee is allowed to shout DOUBLE EVENT! at you without wearing the joke out in an evening.

Which events are allowed to share a stage

Not any two. There is a compatibility matrix, it is six rules long, and every one of them is there because the combination it forbids would have made the game worse rather than because it was awkward to build.

  1. Never two setbacks. An Oven Hiccup (×0.4) inside a Clot (×0.5) is production at a fifth for over a minute. That is not weather, it is a punishment for a coincidence you had no part in. One thing can be going wrong at a time.
  2. Never two events that want the stage. Every clickable event scatters real buttons over the same surface the hero cookie is on. Two at once means rain drops covering sprinkles, a delivery chain whose next parcel is hidden behind a comet, and clicks landing on whichever button was drawn last. There is one stage, so at most one event uses it.
  3. A choice event is always alone. The Taste Test puts a question and two buttons in the middle of the stage and then waits for an answer. Weighing a tradeoff while a comet crosses the screen is not a decision, it is noise — and the card would be sitting on top of the other event’s targets. This is the one rule that is about attention rather than about arithmetic.
  4. The Mouse Raid is never in a stack. It is not drawn from the pool at all, it can take 80% of your balance, and it owns the stage completely. It stays exactly as alone as it has always been.
  5. No event stacks with itself. Two Sugar Rushes would be a ×49 nobody designed, and the HUD would draw the same plate twice with two different clocks on it.
  6. Instant events never stack. A Lucky Crumb pays out and is over inside the tick it arrived in; there is nothing for it to be simultaneous with, and announcing a double event for it would be announcing something that did not happen.

The matrix is symmetric by construction — canStackWith(a, b) and canStackWith(b, a) are the same expression — so there is no draw order in which a forbidden pair slips through. A test asserts that over every ordered pair of every event in the game, and a second test drives the real scheduler over several thousand seeded spawns and checks that no forbidden pair was ever actually rolled.

How a stack is drawn

The size is rolled first, off a single draw against the two thresholds, and only then is the first event picked — from the stackable subset when a stack was rolled, and from the whole pool when it was not. That order is what makes “four per cent of spawns” an exact statement rather than an upper bound: if the first event were drawn from the whole pool, every spawn that happened to land on a Lucky Crumb or a Taste Test would collapse back to a single, and the real rate would be some smaller number nobody had written down.

The remaining slots are filled by rejection sampling — draw at the real weights, refuse anything the matrix forbids, try again — with a budget of eight attempts per slot. Rejection sampling keeps every event at its true weight, where filtering the bag first would bias the result towards whatever survives the filter; the budget is there because a loop with an unbounded worst case is not a scheduler. If all eight attempts miss, the spawn simply ships one event short, which is a spawn you cannot tell from an ordinary one. In practice it effectively never happens: 79% of the pool by weight is stackable and only 31% of it is clickable.

What it looks like while it is running

The marquee announces the headline and then the names, with every event’s emblem beside them — not their descriptions. Two or three descriptions run together is a paragraph, and the marquee has six seconds. Each event then gets its own plate in the HUD, carrying its own accent, its own name and its own countdown, because the members of a stack run on separate clocks: a six-second Burnt Batch Frenzy beside a ninety-second Baker’s Dozen is not one event with two durations, and the frenzy’s plate disappears eighty-four seconds before its companion’s.

Three plates do not push the rest of the cabinet down. At two the plates drop their countdown bar and keep the number of seconds — the number is the fact, the bar is a picture of it — and at three they also lose their vertical padding and shrink their emblem. Nothing that carries information is ever dropped: the name and the seconds are on every plate at every size, and each plate’s accessible name is identical at all three. The compression rule is a plain function of the count, so it is under test without needing a browser.

The six events added with them

A bigger bag is what makes a stack interesting, so double events shipped alongside six new faces. Each earns its place by being worth a different amount to a different player, or by asking for a different gesture — a sixth “here is a lump of cookies” would only have made the pool longer.

  • Cookie Eclipse is Cookie Rain inverted. The rain puts twelve identical targets up and the correct play is to spray clicks at the stage, which is a wrist rather than a skill; the eclipse puts up five, worth about three times as much each, on a stage that has gone dark. Finding them is the gesture. The dimming is a scrim over the game stage only — the counter, the per-second plate, the shop and the HUD are all outside it, so no number in the application loses a single point of contrast for sixteen seconds. An event that made prices hard to read would be an accessibility regression wearing a costume.
  • Crumb Comet is the pool’s one pure skill prize, and it exists because Cookie Rain was slowed to a genuinely catchable pace in the previous lane. That made the rain friendlier and took the last of the pressure out of the clickable family, so this puts a single hard catch back: one big object crossing the stage exactly once, worth about nine rain drops if you get it and nothing if you do not. It is the only clickable event that pays nothing on expiry — fair only because it is rare and because missing costs you nothing that was already yours. It is deliberately the largest target in the game, because it is a test of timing, not of aim.
  • Baker’s Dozen was sketched as “a window where the thirteenth purchase is free”. That was tried and rejected: counting to thirteen means the shop’s printed prices stop being what you pay, and it means tracking a counter the game would then have to display, argue about and save. The rebate keeps the fiction exactly — one cookie in thirteen comes back — and pays it continuously through the same seam Market Day uses. Spend thirteen farms’ worth during the window and you have had precisely one farm free, and no price anywhere printed a lie.
  • Static Cling is described above with the other setbacks. It is the pool’s first click-side penalty.
  • Grandma Convention is worth a fact about your save rather than a fact about the pool. A Production Frenzy is ×7 of everything to everybody; the Convention is ×4 of your grandmas and your farms and nothing else, so it is enormous on a save built on the early generators and close to nothing on one that went straight to portals. It is stated as a surge on named generator ids rather than as a global multiplier with a footnote, which is what makes it structurally honest: the ids are on the event’s own definition, this page prints them, and exactly those and nothing else are surged.
  • Overtime Crew is the only event that touches a subgame, and the only one that can be worth literally nothing. If a room is under construction the crew serves three minutes of building in one; if the site is quiet, the event happens, is announced, and does nothing. That is not a bug and the copy says so: an event that silently became a production boon when it had nothing to speed up would be two events sharing one name, and you would never work out which one you had. The honest version of “sometimes useless” is telling people when.

The last two reach outside the pool by id string only. The events module names grandma, farm and home as strings and never imports the modules that own them; the composition layers do the reaching. An id nothing recognises surges nothing and speeds up nothing, which is the honest failure — the event still happens, still announces itself, and is worth what you actually own.

The Mouse Raid

Roughly once an hour, mice get onto the counter. Three to five of them scurry across the game stage for twenty seconds, each one a real button you can whack. Every mouse still loose when the twenty seconds are up carries cookies away with it, and if all of them get away the raid takes eighty per cent of your current balance. Whack every one and it takes nothing at all, and pays you a small bonus for the trouble.

One fault this frame found by being looked at. Read the red plate: it says 6s5 of 5 mice left. In the single-plate layout the countdown and the mice count are printed with nothing between them, so the seconds run straight into the count — the same class of fault as the Night Shift38s one the double-event lane found and fixed in the two-plate layout, recurring in the layout beside it. It is named here rather than cropped out, and it is not fixed in this release.

What it takes, exactly

The theft is balance × 0.8 × escaped / total, so one mouse of five getting away costs sixteen per cent and all five cost the full eighty. It is scaled rather than all-or-nothing on purpose: a raid you nearly stopped should cost you nearly nothing, and whacking four of five visibly buys back four fifths of your balance.

It takes cookies, not history. The theft comes off your balance and never off your lifetime total or your baked-cookies statistic. Those two are the record of what this save has ever produced; they gate achievements and the prestige projection, and a mouse eating a biscuit does not un-bake it. Letting a raid rewind them would make it quietly retroactive — revoking achievements and ascension points hours after the fact — which is a far worse punishment than the one you agreed to. So a raid is expensive and it is survivable, and nothing it does can move a number that only ever goes up.

Fat mice, and hit points

Most mice go down to a single whack. Raids of four or five bring one fat mouse, which takes two or three hits and carries double an ordinary mouse’s share of the theft — so a raid of four ordinary mice plus a fat one splits into six shares, and the fat one getting away on its own costs a third of the ceiling rather than a fifth of it. The head count you see in the aftermath card still counts heads, because that is what you watched happen; the split underneath is by share, and this is where that is written down.

A fat mouse is drawn larger and carries a row of pips showing how many hits it has left, and its accessible name says the same thing in words.

Raid supplies

Three consumables, bought with cookies, by hand, one at a time, from a stock that is capped — three of each to begin with, and more once you have paid for the shelf space. They are insurance, not immunity, and a save that never buys one plays exactly the game that shipped before them. Each is priced from a base that multiplies by four for every one you have ever bought, so spending your stock does not reset the price.

A pass is not a defence, and the game does not pretend it is. Whacking every mouse yourself pays the defended bonus and spends no pass — a pass in the drawer is never wasted on a raid you were going to win. When a pass is spent, the aftermath card says so in as many words (“A Whack Pass was spent”) and the announcement says it too, rather than congratulating you on mice you never touched.

The two whack consumables arm themselves at the start of a raid if you have them in stock. That is deliberate: an “arm this now?” prompt in the middle of a twenty-second window would be a worse game and a much worse experience for anyone who needs a moment to read it. They are spent by that raid whatever happens, because what they buy is a raid that is easier to fight, and you got one. Stock is a plain count rather than a list of individual items, so there is no oldest pass to spend first — there is nothing to sort.

The shelf itself is the shop. Each of the three plates in the HUD is a real buy control in the same coin-press idiom as the rest of the cabinet: one press buys one, the printed price steps up immediately, a handful of coins flies to the cookie counter when the purchase actually lands, and a press the game refuses — at the cap or short of the price — is announced as a refusal rather than silently ignored. Nothing there is ever hidden or greyed out of the layout: a plate you cannot afford yet still prints its literal price, because a price is information.

Whack Storage

The three-item cap is not a wall, it is the first rung of a ladder. Whack Storage is a small chip on the supplies shelf reading “Storage 3”, and the chip is itself the buy control — a number you want to raise and the button that raises it are the same object, which is one control instead of two and one place to look.

One ladder, not three. Every rung raises the cap on all three consumables at once. Three separate storage tracks would have meant three prices, three progress chips and three things to remember about a mechanic whose entire content is a single number you are trying to raise; one shared shelf says the true thing in one sentence — you can hold three of anything, pay to hold five, pay again to hold eight.

It is stored and priced beside the consumables themselves rather than in the interface-unlock ladder that buys the game’s controls, and the distinction is real: buying a control reveals chrome and leaves the game underneath unchanged, while buying storage changes how much insurance you may carry into a raid. That is a game rule, so it lives with the game rules and it is bought with its own action. The level is a rung index that defaults to zero on read, so a save written before the ladder existed loads as a save at the cap the game always had — nothing to migrate, nothing lost.

Where the reach of a Bigger Whack ends is arithmetic, and it lives in the domain with the rest of the arithmetic: the interface measures where the mice actually are (they are animated, so the layout is the only honest source for that) and a tested pure function decides which of them one swing caught. The domain then checks independently that the raid had a Bigger Whack armed at all, so a swing can never clear a stage it did not pay for.

Rarity, and the rails around it

  • Its own clock, not the pool’s. The raid is never drawn from the weighted bag the other sixteen events come from. It runs on a separate thirty-to-sixty-minute window — rare by the clock rather than rare by the dice — because an eighty-per-cent loss sharing a bag with Lucky Crumb would be either too light to be a raid or too frequent to be fair.
  • Unpredictable inside that window. The gap is drawn uniformly across the whole half-hour band, so at any moment the best guess available is “some time in the next half hour” and no mental clock does better. Nothing in the interface tells you more: there is no countdown, no warning and no readable state before a raid lands — the HUD plate and the mice appear together, at the moment it starts. And the session’s randomness is seeded from real entropy at start-up rather than from a constant, so no two installations share a timeline and a schedule worked out once is a schedule for nobody. Reloading cannot dodge a raid either: the instant the next one is due is saved as a wall-clock time and reloading does not move it.
  • Still one event at a time. It occupies the same single active slot as everything else here, so it can never land on top of a Cookie Rain, and a golden cookie waiting to be clicked still blocks it.
  • Never in a fresh save’s first ten minutes. The opening minutes are the tutorial by another name, and a raid there teaches the wrong lesson.
  • Never below a thousand cookies. Eighty per cent of four hundred cookies is not a robbery, it is noise, and it is noise aimed at exactly the player least able to read a new mechanic. The raid waits rather than re-rolling.
  • Quitting mid-raid settles it against the jar you left behind. Close the app with mice on the counter and the raid is resolved the moment you open it again, before any offline earnings are paid in — so the mice take their share of the balance as it stood when you left, and nothing at all of what accrued while you were away. Force-quitting is not an escape (the raid still costs what it would have cost), and coming back to a night’s progress does not hand the mice eighty per cent of the night. A stocked Whack Pass spends itself here exactly as it would have in play.
  • Never against a window you are not looking at. Nothing about the game clock pauses when the window is hidden — cookies keep accruing exactly as they do while the app is closed, which is what the offline-progress rules already promise — but a raid will not start against a hidden or minimised window. It fires on the first tick after you come back, so the twenty seconds you get are twenty seconds you could actually see.

Unpredictable in play, deterministic in the lab: the scheduler takes its randomness through an injected port, so a test supplies a seed and replays a timeline exactly while the application supplies entropy. The suite drives forty-eight hours of simulated play and asserts that the gaps really do spread across the band rather than clustering on one interval, that every rolled delay lands inside the advertised thirty-to-sixty minutes, that two independently seeded sessions produce different timelines, that no raid ever began inside an exclusion, and that the theft arithmetic is exact for every escaped-mouse count.

The scheduling rules, stated plainly

  • Nothing is ever added to a spawn already running. While anything is running, no roll happens at all. A stack is drawn whole, in one instant, into a clear stage — events only ever leave a busy one. An event appearing next to one you are already working would be indistinguishable from a bug, and it would let a stack outgrow the compatibility rules by accretion.
  • Never over a golden cookie. The golden cookie is a separate system with its own window, but it lands on the same surface, so the event scheduler declines to roll while one is waiting to be clicked.
  • A cooldown, then a window — started by the last event to finish. When the stage clears, a flat quiet minute starts, and only then does a rolled delay of four and a half to thirteen and a half minutes begin. Whatever the dice say, there is always at least a minute of ordinary play between two spawns. Inside a double, clearing the clickable half early does not start that clock while its companion is still running — otherwise playing well would buy you a faster next interruption, and the pacing above would only hold for people who ignored half of every double.
  • Ending it early ends it properly. Catching the last rain drop, crumb or sprinkle, catching the comet, sending the last parcel, thumping the oven, or serving the tray in a Taste Test resolves that event there and then. If it was alone, the cooldown starts; if it had company, its companion carries on for its own remaining time. The reward for answering an event is getting your session back, not a shorter wait for the next one.

Seeded, not random

Like the golden cookie, this scheduler never calls Math.random(). Every roll — when the next event is eligible, which event it is — comes from an injected pseudo-random port whose stream position is saved with the game, so a save-and-reload resumes the same timeline instead of re-rolling your luck. Given the same seed, the whole sequence replays exactly, which is what makes the tests able to drive six hours of simulated play and assert that no forbidden pair of events ever overlapped and no gap was ever shorter than the cooldown.

Time is an argument too. No part of this module reads a clock; the elapsed milliseconds arrive from the same tick the cookies-per-second loop already runs on.

Why Market Day is a rebate and not a discount

Every price in the game is computed in exactly one place, and the Tools tech tree already applies a discount at that seam. Threading a second, timed discount through the same arithmetic would mean the price printed on a shop card and the price actually charged disagreed for sixty seconds at a time. So Market Day does not touch pricing at all: you pay the printed price, and the reducer hands fifteen per cent of it straight back afterwards. The shop stays honest and the effect stays real.

Accessibility

Every clickable event target is a real button with a real accessible name and a hit area comfortably over 44 pixels — never a decorated container with a click handler on it. Each event is announced once, in both languages, through the same throttled status region that already announces achievements and purchases; the marquee on screen is a duplicate of that announcement and is not itself a live region, so nothing is said twice — but it is reachable rather than hidden, because its dismiss button clears real state and a control nobody can reach is worse than no control. Its six-second clock pauses on hover and on focus.

The Taste Test is two ordinary focusable buttons in a card with a name on it; each button’s accessible name states what it pays, including the literal figure on the lump-sum one, and the note under them says outright that letting the clock run out gives you neither. In a Delivery Rush the two parcels that are not next are real buttons carrying disabled, dimmed rather than hidden, and their accessible names say “not this one yet” — a disabled control that explains itself beats an enabled one that silently does nothing.

Under a reduced-motion preference the rain stops moving and becomes twelve still crumbs laid down the same stage, in the same columns, at the same size, worth exactly the same cookies; the sprinkles stop bobbing and stay exactly where they were, at the same 46-pixel size and the same escalating value; the live parcel stops nudging and is marked by its colour and its enabled state alone; the frenzy plate keeps its gold ring and stops pulsing; and the oven chip stops shaking. Nothing becomes unreachable and nothing becomes harder to hit; the only thing that goes away is the motion. No event gates anything: the worst one costs production for thirty seconds and hands you a button to end it sooner.

How it is configured

The spawn window, the cooldown and every payout are fields of one configuration object with a documented default set, not constants buried in the scheduler. Deliberately developer-only configurations exist for capture and smoke runs: one shortens the window to a few seconds, one shortens the raid’s hour instead, and one pins the draw to a single named event so that a 1%-weight frenzy can be photographed honestly rather than waited fifteen hours for. All three are reached by setting a single local-storage key before the game starts and by nothing else, and none of them relaxes a rule the player is subject to — the event that fires is the real event, with its real duration and its real arithmetic. There is no button, no settings row and no in-game control that summons an event, because a random event you can summon is not a random event.
